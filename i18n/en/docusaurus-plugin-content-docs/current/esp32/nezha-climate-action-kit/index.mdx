# Climate Action Kit

The Climate Action Kit helps students ages 8 and up explore local environmental conditions through building, measuring, and programming. Its projects support K–12 science, computing, and engineering lessons, with teacher support adjusted to students' experience.

*Observe your surroundings. Use data to make decisions. Build and test an idea for change.*

Students use environmental sensors to collect readings and program the **Nezha ESP32 controller** to operate fans, gates, and other output devices, also called actuators. They learn how sensor data, programming rules, and physical actions work together in a model. Short classroom observations describe local conditions; climate patterns require evidence collected over much longer periods.

## **Product Introduction**

Designed for STEM / STEAM courses and project-based learning, the kit combines environmental measurements, card-based activities, building-block models, automatic control, and online data displays. RFID (radio-frequency identification) lets the model recognize compatible cards. IoT (Internet of Things) lets the controller send readings over a network to a dashboard.

The kit explores high temperatures, urban heat islands, intense sunlight, flooding caused by heavy rain, weather changes, and low-carbon travel. Students use the Nezha ESP32 multifunctional controller to read onboard and external sensors and an RFID sensor to identify custom cards. Combined with a fan module, motors, a screen, a speaker, and a dedicated IoT platform, these components allow students to build urban climate devices that can “sense—decide—act—record.”

The kit includes **11 projects that build skills step by step and one capstone project**. The featured Smart Low-Carbon Bus Stop combines temperature and humidity sensing, light sensing, a movable shade, a fan, and simulated travel check-ins. Students then apply their earlier work to the Urban Climate Safety Station, testing each subsystem before combining functions.

Students experience the full project-based learning process: identifying problems, collecting data, building models, writing programs, creating automatic responses, and recording and presenting results through IoT. As young urban engineers, they propose climate action solutions that can be tested and verified.

## **Start with Real-World Problems**

High temperatures, intense sunlight, flooding from heavy rain, and urban heat islands are closely connected to everyday life. Students can observe environmental changes through temperature, humidity, water level, light, sound, and air pressure data. They can also use custom RFID cards for interactive activities such as green travel check-ins.

From streets, bus stops, and rooftops to classrooms, community gardens, and low-lying roads, every setting can become a learning space for exploring climate issues. Through observation, building, programming, testing, and presentation, students turn abstract environmental concepts into practical projects with verifiable results.

## **Product Features**

**1\. Start with Onboard Sensors**

The Nezha ESP32 multifunctional controller includes a light sensor, dual noise-reducing microphones, a gyroscope, a speaker, a screen, and RGB LEDs. Students can complete introductory experiments in light sensing, noise observation, orientation detection, and data feedback without connecting external modules.

**2\. From Local Data Display to IoT Dashboards**

Students can first view data on the controller's screen, then upload readings from onboard or external sensors to the dedicated IoT platform. Dashboards allow students to observe changes in light, noise, temperature, water level, or air pressure over time, as well as record RFID green travel check-in counts.

**3\. Connect Sensors and Actuators**

The kit emphasizes actions triggered by data. Students can do more than read sensor values: they can use fan modules and motor-driven structures to create physical responses, such as starting a fan, extending a shade, raising or lowering a gate, or driving an inspection vehicle.

|Environmental change|Data source|Actuator response|
|---|---|---|
|Temperature is too high|Temperature and humidity sensor|The fan module starts to simulate cooling|
|Light is too intense|Onboard light sensor|A motor extends the shade|
|Water level rises|Water level sensor|A motor raises the warning gate or starts the drainage pump model|
|A green travel card is detected|RFID sensor and custom cards|A motor opens the access gate, while the screen displays and counts low-carbon trips|
|Noise is too loud|Onboard dual noise-reducing microphones|The screen displays a prompt and the speaker gives an alert|
|The weather tower tilts|Onboard orientation data supported by the library|The screen shows a tilt warning, with an optional sound|
|Mobile data collection is needed|Multiple sensors|Motors drive a vehicle to collect data in different areas|

**4\. Build Models with Building Blocks**

The kit supports building a variety of urban climate devices with building-block components, turning abstract data into models that students can see, touch, and interact with.

The kit's projects include:

- Urban Light Monitor;

- Urban Noise Monitor;

- Tilt-Alert Weather Tower;

- IoT Urban Microclimate Dashboard;

- Automatic Cooling Fan;

- Smart Shade Station;

- Low-Carbon Travel Check-In Station;

- Smart Low-Carbon Bus Stop;

- Flood Warning Gate;

- Urban Weather Trend Logger;

- Urban Heat Island Explorer;

- Urban Climate Safety Station.

**5\. A Signature Integrated Project**

The Smart Low-Carbon Bus Stop brings a bus stop sign, movable shade, cooling fan, data display, and RFID card scanning area together in one urban bus stop model. Through this complete project, students apply sensor reading, conditional logic, motor control, variable-based counting, and structural design skills.

The bus stop starts its fan when the temperature is too high and extends its shade when the light is too intense. When a green travel card is detected, the screen displays a prompt and increments the check-in count. By combining improved waiting conditions with encouragement for green travel, the project makes climate action concrete, visible, and interactive.

**6\. Task-Driven Learning**

Each project focuses on a real-world problem and guides students through a complete learning cycle:

1. Observe an urban climate or environmental problem;

2. Select suitable onboard or external sensors;

3. Build the corresponding model;

4. Write programs for decision-making and responses;

5. Upload or display real-time data;

6. Record and compare environmental changes;

7. Test the device's performance;

8. Discuss ways to improve the solution;

9. Present the finished project.

## **Module Introduction**

**Nezha ESP32 Multifunctional Controller**

The Nezha ESP32 multifunctional controller is the kit's core controller. It runs programs, reads sensor data, controls the fan module, motors, and screen feedback, and supports uploading data to the dedicated IoT platform.

The controller includes a screen, a light sensor, dual noise-reducing microphones, a gyroscope, a speaker, programmable buttons, and RGB LEDs, making it suitable for progressing from basic sensor experiments to integrated projects.

**Fan Module**

The fan module can be driven directly by a GPIO pin to simulate urban cooling, ventilation, and airflow. Students can control when the fan starts and stops based on temperature or other experimental conditions.

**RFID Sensor and Custom Cards**

The RFID sensor reads identification information from custom cards to simulate green travel check-ins, identity verification, or climate action task triggers. Students can program responses to card identification results, controlling gates, motors, the screen, RGB LEDs, or the speaker.

**Motors**

Motors drive shades, gates, drainage models, and mobile exploration vehicles, allowing students' projects to progress from displaying data to taking automatic action.

## **Featured Project**

**Smart Low-Carbon Bus Stop**

The Smart Low-Carbon Bus Stop combines urban climate improvements with green travel. Students build a bus stop base, sign, movable shade, cooling fan, data display area, and RFID card scanning area, enabling the model to respond automatically to environmental changes.

|Learning question|How can low-carbon travel be made more comfortable in hot, sunny urban environments?|
|---|---|
|Environmental sensing|The temperature and humidity sensor measures temperature, while the onboard light sensor measures light levels|
|Automatic responses|Start the fan when the temperature is too high; extend the shade when the light is too intense|
|Interactive functions|Identify RFID green travel cards, display prompts, and count check-ins|
|Key concepts|Urban heat islands, green travel, sensors and actuators, conditional logic, variable-based counting, thresholds and hysteresis, and mechanical transmission|
|Suggested difficulty|Upper intermediate|
|Suggested duration|1–2 lessons|
|Extension ideas|Upload temperature, light, and green travel counts to the IoT platform; design a points system for low-carbon travel|

With the recognizable features of an urban public facility, a wide range of functions, and intuitive interactions, this project is suitable for classroom teaching, demonstration lessons, club activities, and science and technology festival displays.

## **Capstone Project**

**Urban Climate Safety Station**

The Urban Climate Safety Station brings together the skills learned in earlier projects. Students combine temperature and humidity, water level, light, orientation, air pressure, and RFID inputs with fan control, shading, warnings, and optional IoT records. Sound monitoring can be added from Case 02 if the tested design supports it.

Students decide how tasks should work together and which task takes priority when an output device is shared. Before connecting modules, check the available ports, bus addresses, library support, and power requirements. If the complete design exceeds the controller's resources, build it in stages or divide it into cooperating classroom stations.

## **Teaching and Learning Support**

Choose the level of support by students' experience, rather than age alone. The following are suggested ways to adapt the same projects:

|Learners|Suggested support|Evidence of learning|
|---|---|---|
|Primary / elementary, ages 8+|Begin with Cases 01–03. Provide a teacher-checked starter program, model the measurement steps, and compare one condition at a time|A labeled model, a small results table, and an explanation of what changed|
|Lower secondary / middle school|Let students choose thresholds from measurements, add external modules, and test each branch of a decision|A program, repeated measurements, and a record of one design improvement|
|Upper secondary / high school or experienced clubs|Extend the integrated projects with data analysis, separate task timers, error handling, and peer testing|Graphs, a test plan covering combined conditions, and an explanation of limitations|

In small groups, rotate the roles of builder, programmer, tester, and recorder. Start each investigation with a prediction. Record the location, time, input reading, expected response, and observed response, then use the results to explain any changes to the design.

**Before a lesson:** check the hardware and current library support, prepare any required network account, and test the model's stopping and reset behavior. Most case pages currently provide a logic guide and image placeholders rather than a verified downloadable program; allow time to prepare and test a working version.

**Use evidence carefully:** light and sound readings are relative unless units and calibration are confirmed. Fans move air and do not necessarily lower room temperature. RFID check-ins simulate travel choices and do not measure carbon savings. Air pressure trends do not provide certain weather forecasts. The warning models are classroom demonstrations and are not validated for real safety decisions.

## **Learning Outcomes**

Through environmental observation, data collection, model building, programming, and presentation, students can:

- Understand environmental data such as temperature, humidity, light, water level, sound, orientation, and air pressure;
- Develop programming skills in sensor reading, conditional logic, variables, loops, and motor and fan control;
- Learn about climate action topics such as urban heat islands, rainwater management, and low-carbon travel;
- Apply engineering design methods to build structures, test functions, and improve solutions;
- Observe trends through IoT dashboards and use data to explain project performance;
- Present their urban climate action solutions through model demonstrations, programs, and data.

From sensing the environment to taking action, the Climate Action Kit helps students understand climate through making and explore change through programming.
