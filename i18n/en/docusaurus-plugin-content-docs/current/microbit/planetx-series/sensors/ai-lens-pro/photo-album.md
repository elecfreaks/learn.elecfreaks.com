# Case Study: AI Image Observation Exhibition

Browse your photos and choose a set that explains your experiment

## Introduction

How can you turn photos into useful observation records? Use the AI Lens Pro photo album to browse the images saved in the Camera Observation Diary, compare their shooting conditions, and write short captions for a selected set.

The album displays saved images. In this project, record groups and captions on paper or in a computer document; automatic classification and on-device captions are not required.

<!-- Image placeholder: observation exhibition and photo caption cards -->

## Learning Objectives

1. Open the album, switch between photos, and verify saved images.
2. Match each photo to its shooting record.
3. Explain image differences using observable details.
4. Back up original photos before organizing them.

## Hardware Connection

1. Charge AI Lens Pro and check that the storage containing the photos is available.
2. If photos are on a TF card, turn off the device, check that the card is installed, and then power on.
3. Operate the camera directly; an external micro:bit is not required.

## Software Setup

Open **Photo Album** from the AI Lens Pro main menu. If there are no photos yet, first complete the shooting activity in the [Camera Observation Diary](./camera.md).

The list view, photo navigation, and available actions depend on your firmware. Confirm that you can open an existing photo before organizing your collection.

## Using the Features

### 1. Browse and Check Photos

View each photo and match its displayed name or browsing order to your record sheet. Check the image content too; list order alone does not establish the shooting conditions.

### 2. Compare Observable Details

Compare photos around one question, such as how the subject's share of the frame changes with distance. If side-by-side viewing is unavailable, switch between photos and take notes, or export them to a computer.

### 3. Keep the Originals

Back up photos you need before organizing them. If deletion is available, check the file and verify that its backup opens before deleting anything. This activity does not require deleting photos.

## Example Activity: Select Four Photos for an Exhibition

### How It Works

Find four photos of the same object, check their conditions, and choose images that clearly show the effects of distance, angle, and lighting. Write a caption card for each image.

### Record Settings

| Photo | Conditions to check | Caption card content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reference | Front view, even lighting, reference distance | Subject name and reference conditions |
| Distance comparison | Only distance increased | Change in the subject's share of the frame |
| Angle comparison | Only object orientation changed | Changes in visible outline and detail |
| Lighting comparison | Only lighting changed | Changes in brightness and perceived color |

### Steps

1. Open the album, find the reference photo, and record its displayed filename or recognizable features.
2. Find the three comparison photos and check each against the shooting record.
3. Write one sentence explaining what changed and one explaining what you observed for each photo.
4. Present the photos in the order reference, distance, angle, and lighting. Keep the captions on external cards.
5. To share them, export the originals using [Remote File Management](./remote-file-management.md), then arrange the exhibition on a computer.

<!-- Image placeholder: four photos with observation caption cards -->

**Activity Notes:** Album photos are static records. They are not the current camera view and do not automatically trigger live recognition. If you cannot match a photo to its conditions, check your shooting notes or take it again.

## Save and Check

1. Check that every selected image opens and has a matching record.
2. When exporting, verify the file count and open the images on the computer.
3. Keep the originals and add text or crop copies so you can compare the originals again later.

## Questions and Extensions

1. Why can a photo alone be insufficient to establish which conditions changed?
2. How does choosing the most attractive photo differ from choosing the most informative one?
3. What shooting problems can blurred or overly dark photos reveal?
4. Can you combine several sets into a class image observation handbook?
